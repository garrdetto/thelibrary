How to use

  • Left-drag to rotate, right-drag to pan, scroll to zoom. The view buttons jump to set angles.
  • Setup: change weight, springs, bars, bump springs, scales, and lateral and long g. The car rolls, pitches and heaves, and the colored disc under each tire grows with its load.
  • Test on track drives this setup on a short oval built from your corner radius and banking, in Short Track Showdown's Super Late Model at Thrill Park Speedway. Springs, bump springs, bars, ride heights, scales, stagger and the link geometry all change how it handles: watch it bottom out, lift the nose, roll and move weight, let the test driver run laps or drive it yourself, then replay any lap from several angles at once.
  • Drive the corner (Setup, Track) loops the car through braking, turn-in, mid-corner and exit so you can watch the loads change. Motion ×5 makes small body movements easier to see.
  • Kinematics: pick LF or RF, then move the upper A-arm bar, lower arm mounts, slugs, and shims. Rear moves the 3rd link, lower links and track bar, swaps the track bar for a J-Bar, or mounts either one reversed. Use Heave, Roll, or Sweep to move the suspension and watch camber, toe, the roll centers and rear steer.
  • Plots: every kinematic curve, front and rear, through wheel travel, body roll and steering. Dashed lines are the baseline: the saved setup you last loaded or saved (the car as modeled until you do). Orange numbers on the Kinematics tab show which parts changed length.
  • Saved setups: save, load, and compare setups side by side. Export a setup to a file to share it.
  • Load chassis hardpoints runs the math on another car's pivot points. Download the current ones first to see the file format.
  • Click a section heading to fold it away. The camera stays where you put it when you switch tabs.
  • Hover any underlined term for a short definition.

Assumptions to check

From your straight rail CAD: front double A-arms with the strut-rod style lower arm (front leg + 14.75 in aft link), screw-in ball joints, 8.5 in steering arms on a centerlink, coilovers on the lower arms; rear 3-link: LR lower link 16 in, RR lower link 20 in, 3rd link on top of the center section, track bar from the left side of the housing to the chassis on the right; coilovers behind the axle; front bar 1.75 OD × 1.125 ID, about 38 in between arms, 9.6 in arms. The CAD car is not posed on flat ground (RF 0.8 in high, rear end rolled 2.2° and steered 1.4°), so the lab re-seats it: each front corner moves until its tire touches the ground, and the rear end is set square, level and centered at ride height with the link lengths set to match, the way you'd square it with the link adjusters and track bar. The ground is where the LF and LR tires sit in the CAD; at that stance the frame rails are 3.68 in up with a rigid 26.6 in tire. Spring motion ratios come from the coilover eye points through travel, front and rear. Bar MR 0.65: end link travel per inch of wheel travel from the CAD end link points is LF 0.69, RF 0.66. Still assumed: G = 11.5e6 psi, tire rate (default 1,300 lb/in), chassis twist stiffness (default 4,000 ft-lb/°). The front and rear each roll against their own springs and bars, joined by the frame's twist stiffness. Front roll stiffness = (kLF+kRF)·MR²·(t/2)² + bar; rear = (kLR+kRR)·MR²·(base/2)² + rear bar. A 3-link on rod ends adds no roll stiffness of its own. Bar = (G·J/L)·(t·MRbar/arm)², J = π(OD⁴−ID⁴)/32. Load transfer per axle = sprung W·Ay·hrc/t + K·φ/t + unsprung W·Ay·13.3/t. Roll, both roll centers and both jacking forces are solved together. The rear roll center is found from the whole linkage: the body is rolled a hair about the axle and the point the axle swings about, seen from the front, is the roll center. Rear jacking = lateral force × how far the axle moves sideways per inch of bump, from the same linkage. Rear steer, pinion change and wheelbase change come straight from solving the axle's position with all four link lengths held. Anti-squat uses the side-view instant center of each lower link with the 3rd link (averaged left and right), from the rear contact patch. Ride heights: tire loaded radius = rollout ÷ 2π − load ÷ tire rate. In Standard mode you enter three ride heights (the frame is rigid, so the fourth follows) and the lab takes the tires out to find where the suspension sits. In Advanced mode the springs, shocks and tires find the ride heights and cross: the frame rises, pitches and rolls until the four spring forces plus unsprung weight carry the scale weight with the given left and rear percentages. Wedge and bar preload are solved as a rigid chassis on four springs plus the bar. Bump springs add their rate once shock travel passes the gap. Balance uses a simple tire whose friction falls with load (μ = 1.5 − 0.0004·Fz); it ignores camber, stagger and rear steer. Stagger needed = rear tire rollout × rear track ÷ corner radius. Long g moves W·g·h/WB between the axles; the springs see it less the anti-dive (front, braking), anti-lift (rear, braking) or anti-squat (throttle). Shocks aren't modeled: each moment is a steady state. Banking θ (inside low): lateral g is the level cornering g ay; across the track the car feels ay·cosθ − sinθ, and every tire is pressed in by cosθ + ay·sinθ times its static load.

Every plot is the geometry as you have it on the Kinematics tab (solid) against the baseline (dashed): the saved setup you last loaded or saved, or the car as modeled until you load or save one. The dotted vertical line is where the car sits now: move it with Heave, Roll, Steer and the g sliders above.

Jacking plots use the lateral g from Move the car, or 1 g when that is 0. Anti-dive, anti-squat and anti-lift use the Setup tab's CG height and front brake %.

Arm lengths and position

Upper arm fwd is how most cars set caster: spacers move the whole arm on its cross shaft, so the arm angles don't change. Its readout shows the caster that results.

Ball joints and shims

Camber shim moves the upper ball joint out (+) or in (−). Caster shim moves it back (+) or forward (−), and its readout shows the caster angle that results — positive is the upper ball joint behind the lower.

The orange line through the two ball joints is the kingpin axis; the gray dashed line is vertical. The angle between them, seen from the side, is caster.

Steering knuckle

Both mounts down by the same amount is a raised snout: the ball joints sit lower at the same ride height, so the arms angle down and the roll center moves. Upper up or lower down makes the knuckle taller. Static toe is reset; camber and caster show what the arms did, so re-shim if you want them back.

Steering

Steering is applied to both corners and to the scales: with caster, turning the wheel raises one front corner and lowers the other, which moves cross weight. That is what a caster split is for.

3rd link

Moving an end keeps the axle where it is at ride height (the link length follows). The link angle, with the lower links, sets anti-squat and how the pinion moves through travel. Sliding the link sideways only matters when it is angled: the up or down part of its pull, off center, leans on one rear tire more than the other.

Lower links

Link lengths follow so the axle stays square at ride height. Different link angles left and right steer the axle as it rolls and moves up and down.

How this is solved Each front corner is a double A-arm. The lower ball joint swings about the lower inner pivot axis; the upper ball joint swings about the upper inner pivot axis and must stay a fixed kingpin length from the lower; the outer tie rod end must stay a fixed tie rod length from the inner end, which sets steer. The knuckle, hub, and wheel ride on those three points. When you move a pivot, the wheel stays at its ride-height position and the arm, kingpin, and tie rod lengths change to fit. Camber and caster shims, upper arm length and the upper arm slide rotate the knuckle about the lower ball joint; lower arm length rotates it about the upper ball joint; then toe is reset with the tie rod. The front spring motion ratio comes from the coilover eye points in your CAD: the lower eye rides on the lower arm, and spring travel per inch of wheel travel is worked out through the whole range, so the wheel rate changes with travel. Roll moves the wheels ±(track/2)·φ relative to the body; camber is reported to the ground. Anti-dive = tan θ · (wheelbase / CG height) · front brake %, where θ is the side-view instant center angle from the contact patch. Front jacking: each front tire's lateral force (its load × lateral g) acts along the line from its contact patch to its front-view instant center, so its vertical part on the chassis is Fy × (rise ÷ run) of that line. Front ride change in brackets = total ÷ ((LF + RF spring rate) × MR²), springs only; the Setup tab's Ride F / R also counts bump springs. Under braking or throttle, the share of the weight transfer the arms carry (anti-dive at the front, truck arm anti-lift or anti-squat at the rear) is added to jacking. Rear: the axle is a solid body on four rod-end links (two lower links, the 3rd link and the track bar). For any bump and roll of the axle the lab solves its fore-aft and sideways position, pinion angle and steer so all four links keep their lengths. At ride height it sits square, level and centered. The rear roll center is the point the axle swings about, seen from the front, when the body rolls; rear jacking = (rear weight − rear unsprung) × lateral g × the axle's sideways move per inch of bump. Rear spring motion ratio comes from the rear shock eyes through the same solve. Pivot points were taken from the centers of your ball joint, spherical bearing, slug and tie rod parts.
